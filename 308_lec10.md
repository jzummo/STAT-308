Review of CLT: $X_i = g(\epsilon_i, \dots)$ where $\epsilon_i$ iid. Define $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n - n \E[X_1]$. Assuming either
$$
\sum \|P_0 X_i\| < \infty
$$
or
$$
\sum \|X_i - X_i^*\| < \infty
$$
then
$$
\frac{S_n}{\sqrt n} \Rightarrow \N(0, \sigma^2_\infty).
$$
Today we'll see the CLT for SGD estimates. Recall the setting
$$
\theta_i = \theta_{i-1} - \gamma g(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)
$$
where $X_i$ is iid. Here, $\gamma > 0$ is the learning rate. Define $m(\theta) = \E[g(\theta, X_1)]$. The conditions we require are

- Strong convexity: $\langle m(\theta) - m(\theta'), \theta - \theta' \rangle \geq \mu |\theta - \theta'|^2$ for $\mu > 0$
- Lipschitz continuity: $\|g(\theta, X_1) - g(\theta', X_1)\|_p \leq L_p \|\theta - \theta'\|_p$

Let $F_{X_i}^\gamma$ be the transition map. We derived the following geometric moment contraction bound:
$$
\|F_{X_i}(\theta) - F_{X_i}(\theta')\|_p \leq (1 - \gamma \kappa)|\theta - \theta'|
$$
The derivation is easy to do for the case where $p = 2$.

**Rio's inequality**: for $p \geq 2$ and random variables $V, W$ satisfying $\E[V \mid W] = 0$, we have
$$
\|W + V\|_p^2 \leq \|W\|_p^2 + (p - 1)\|V\|_p^2
$$
As a consequence, for a martingale difference sequence $D_i$,
$$
\|D_1 + \cdots + D_n\|_p^2 \leq (p - 1)\sum_{i=1}^n \|D_i\|_p^2
$$
This is related to the Burkholder-Davis-Grundy inequality.

To prove GMC for $p \geq 2$, we can use Rio's inequality in conjunction with the assumptions
$$
\theta - \gamma g(\theta, X) = \theta - \gamma m(\theta) + \gamma (m(\theta) - g(\theta, X))
$$
Note that $\E[m(\theta) - g(\theta, X) \mid \theta] = 0$ by definition of the expectation. Applying Rio's inequality,
$$
\|\theta - \gamma g(\theta, X) - \theta' + \gamma g(\theta', X)\|_p^2 \leq \cdots
$$
**TODO FINISH** using the assumptions and the $p = 2$ case,
$$
\leq |(1 - \gamma \kappa)(\theta - \theta')|^2 + (p - 1)\gamma^2(2L_p(\theta - \theta'))^2
$$
Therefore,
$$
\E|\theta - \theta_i'|^p = O(\rho^i), \qquad \rho = 1 - \gamma C
$$
Thus, $\theta_i$ has summable physical dependence and thus satisfies a CLT.

This is related to algorithmic stability:
$$
\theta_n = F_n(X_n, \dots X_1), \qquad \theta_n' = F_n(X_n, \dots, X_1')
$$
Then
$$
\|\theta_n - \theta_n'\|_p^2 \leq (1 - \gamma C)^n \|X_i - X_i'\|_p^2
$$
Is this the end of the story? In practice, $\gamma$ is chosen to be very small. For example, $\gamma = n^{-5/12}$ is common. In this setting where $\gamma \rightarrow 0$ but $n \gamma \rightarrow \infty$, the above CLT does not apply.

Performance of SGD with small $\gamma$:
$$
\rho^i \approx (1 - \gamma C)^i,
$$
we need $i >> 1/\gamma$ to achieve a diminishing rate. The isuse is that when $i$ is small, $\theta_i$ is heavily autocorrelated.

Suppose $\theta^* = 0$ so that $m(\theta^*) = 0$. Then $\theta_n$ is a distribution which is non-degenerate in general. Only when $\gamma \rightarrow 0$ can $\theta_\infty$ concentrate on $0$, the true parameter of interest. Let $\theta_\infty(\gamma)$ denote the limiting marginal distribution.

**Theorem**: under suitable conditions,
$$
\E[\theta_\infty(\gamma)] = \theta^* + A_1 \gamma + A_2 \gamma^2 + O(\gamma^{2.5}).
$$
For fixed $\gamma$, we can estimate
$$
\hat \theta_\infty(\gamma) = \frac{\theta_1 + \cdots + \theta_n}{n}
$$
How can we estimate $\theta^*$ then? We can use
$$
2\hat\theta_\infty(\gamma) - \hat\theta_\infty(2\gamma) \\
= 2(\theta^* + A_1 \gamma + A_2 \gamma^2 + O(\gamma^{2.5})) - (\theta^* + 2A_1 \gamma + 4A_2 \gamma^2 + O(\gamma^{2.5})) \\
= \theta^* - 2A_2 \gamma^2 + O(\gamma^{2.5})
$$
In numerical analysis, this is called Richardson-Romberg extrapolation. In statistics, we call this jackknife estimation.

Next, we'll discuss a CLT for the limiting regime $\gamma \rightarrow 0$. It turns out that
$$
\frac{\theta_\infty(\gamma) - \theta^*}{\sqrt \gamma} \Rightarrow \N(0, \Gamma), \quad \text{ as } \gamma \rightarrow 0.
$$
**Proof**
Assume $\theta^* = 0$ as usual. Then
$$
\E[\theta_i^2] = \E[\theta_{i-1}^2 - 2\gamma g(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)\theta_{i-1} + \gamma^2 g^2(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)] \\
= \E[\theta_{i-1}^2] - 2 \gamma \theta_{i-1}m(\theta_{i-1}) + \gamma^2 \E[g^2(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)] \\
\leq (1 - \gamma)\E[\theta_{i-1}^2] + \gamma^2 \kappa
$$
Repeating the recursion, we get
$$
\E[\theta_i^2] \leq \frac{\gamma^2 \kappa}{\gamma} = \gamma \kappa
$$
Thus $\E[\theta_i^2] = O(\gamma)$. This gives us hope to prove the CLT (i.e. the scaling wrt $\gamma$ is correct).
$$
\theta_i = \theta_{i-1} - \gamma g(\theta_{i-1}, X_i) \\
= \theta_{i-1} - \gamma m(\theta_{i-1}) + \gamma D(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)
$$
where $D(\theta_{i-1}, X_i) = m(\theta_{i-1}) - g(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)$. Note that $D(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)$ is a martingale difference sequence wrt the filtration generated by the $\theta_i$.
$$
= \theta_{i-1} - \gamma[\theta_{i-1}m'(0) + R_{i-1}] + \gamma D(0, X_i) + \gamma U(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)
$$
where $R_i$ is the remainder from first-order Taylor expansion and
$$
U(\theta_{i-1}, X_i) = D(\theta_{i-1}, X_i) - D(0, X_i)
$$
this is called the *canonical form* of SGD. Observe that $D(0, X_i)$ is iid and $U(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)$ is still a martingale difference. Moreover, this $U(\theta_{i-1}, X_i)$ term is negligible. Thus, we have constructed the linear approximation
$$
\theta_i^L = \theta_{i-1}^L - \gamma \theta_{i-1}^L m'(0) + \gamma D(0, X_i)
$$
Let $\eta_i = D(0, X_i)$.  Without loss of generality, let $m'(0) = 1$. This process is
$$
\theta_i^L = (1 - \gamma)\theta_{i-1}^L + \gamma \eta_i^L
$$
This is a bona fide AR(1). Since $\gamma$ is small, it behaves like a unit root process. Unraveling the recursion,
$$
\theta_i^L = \gamma \eta_i + (1 - \gamma) \gamma \eta_{i-1} + (1 - \gamma)^2 \gamma \eta_{i-2} + \cdots
$$
How do we prove a CLT for this? Since the terms in the infinite summation are not identically distributed, we need to use Lindeberg-Feller:
$$
S_n = W_{n1} + \cdots + W_{nk_n}, \qquad \sigma^2_{nk} = \E[W_{nk}^2], \quad \sum_{k=1}^{k_n} \sigma_{nk}^2 = 1
$$
Assume

1. $\max_{k \leq k_n} \sigma^2_{nk} \rightarrow 0$
2. $\sum_{k=1}^{k_n} W_{nk}^2 \one[|W_{nk} > \epsilon] \rightarrow 0$ as $n \rightarrow 0$ for all $\epsilon > 0$.

Then Lindeberg-Feller gives us the CLT.

In our setting, we are dealing with
$$
\sum_{i=-\infty}^\infty \eta_i c_{i\gamma}, \quad \sum_{i=-\infty}^\infty c_{i\gamma}^2 = 1
$$
Now it's easy to verify the conditions of the Lindeberg-Feller theorem.

An interesting tidbit: Lindeberg says that 1. and 2. imply the CLT, and Feller says that 1. and CLT imply 2. Wu recently proved that CLT + $\eta_i$ non-normal implies 1. However, if $\eta_i$ is not normal, then 1. is not needed.
